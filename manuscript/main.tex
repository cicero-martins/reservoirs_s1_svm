The area-to-perimeter ratio (A/P) of a reservoir shoreline, computable in
advance from global databases, predicts how accurately Sentinel-1 can monitor
its surface area. We test this in a single cloud-based Google Earth Engine
pipeline that compares per-scene water detectors: a single-polarisation VV Otsu
threshold and a dual-polarisation (VV+VH) support vector machine (SVM)
re-trained for each acquisition, with training samples drawn automatically from
the JRC Global Surface Water dataset. The comparison covers 62 reservoirs on
five continents, referenced to JRC surface water and screened for reference
reliability, plus four Sicilian reservoirs validated against 3~m PlanetScope
near-truth. A/P is a strong predictor of surface-area skill (Spearman
$\rho = 0.51$, $p = 3\times10^{-5}$): it sets a geometric ceiling, high and
stable for compact reservoirs (median KGE 0.93 above 200~m) and lower and more
variable for narrow, dendritic ones (0.48 below 100~m). The second
polarisation adds no reliable accuracy across this global set: the single-band
per-scene Otsu is as accurate as the dual-band SVM, wins in most reservoirs,
and is the simplest and cheapest to run, whereas a fixed-training SVM is the
least accurate, so per-scene adaptivity matters more than polarisation.
Climate is a genuine secondary modulator, humid and vegetated reservoirs
underperforming at given A/P even after every reservoir with an unreliable
optical reference is excluded. We release the workflow as an interactive
Google Earth Engine application that auto-trains from JRC and runs this
per-scene detector over more than 35,000 global reservoirs (Global Dam Watch),
a reliable, low-cost, and transferable basis for the surface-area input to
reservoir storage monitoring in data-scarce regions.
\end{abstract}
