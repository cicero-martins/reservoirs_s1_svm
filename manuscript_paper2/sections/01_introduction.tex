\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reservoirs regulate water supply, irrigation, hydropower and flood risk
\citep{Pokhrel2021}, and their operation rests on knowing how much water is stored at a
given level, the area--elevation--volume (AEV) relationship. That relationship decays as
sediment delivered by the contributing basin settles on the reservoir floor and
progressively consumes capacity, at rates that have already cut global storage in large
reservoirs by several per cent and are projected to outpace new construction
\citep{Wisser2013}; in Italy, half of fifty surveyed reservoirs are expected to have lost
half their design capacity by 2050 \citep{Patro2022}. Recovering the true relationship
means resurveying the bed, an operation costly enough that most reservoirs are surveyed
once in a generation, if at all, and the in-situ records that might otherwise track the
change are themselves sparse, declining, and frequently withheld for strategic or
commercial reasons \citep{BuskerEtAl2019, Zhang2026}. Between surveys, a reservoir is
therefore operated on a curve known to be wrong by an unknown amount.

Satellite remote sensing can supply both ingredients the AEV relationship requires, and two
recent developments make it possible to supply them continuously and without cost.
Water-surface \emph{elevation} comes from satellite altimetry, from the nadir radar missions
harmonised in databases such as DAHITI \citep{Schwatke2015} to, since 2022, the wide-swath
mission SWOT \citep{Biancamaria2016}, which measures elevation across a swath rather than
along a single ground track. Water-surface \emph{area} is mapped routinely from both optical
and radar imagery, and the trade-off between them is documented. Optical sensors discriminate
water through its distinctive spectral response and underpin the longest global surface-water
records \citep{Pekel2016}, and they are the more accurate of the two per observation, but they
observe only under cloud-free daylight and therefore less often \citep{PenaLuque2021}. SAR
measures backscatter instead, which is largely unaffected by cloud cover and solar
illumination \citep{Torres2012, Mullissa2021} though sensitive to wind-driven surface
roughness \citep{McQuillan2025}, trading some per-scene accuracy for an acquisition that is
essentially guaranteed. Reconstructing a curve favours that trade, because the method needs
waterlines distributed across a drawdown rather than any single accurate outline, and in
Mediterranean and tropical settings cloud cover decides whether the drawdown is sampled at
all. SAR water detection is correspondingly well established, from operational flood mapping
\citep{Martinis2015} to systematic surface-water monitoring \citep{Bioresita2018,
PenaLuque2021}, and combining the two sensors is an active line of work
\citep{ValerioEtAl2024, PengEtAl2025}. The free, twice-weekly Sentinel-1 archive
\citep{Torres2012}, processed at scale on platforms such as Google Earth Engine
\citep{Gorelick2017}, delivers this signal with a reliability that a companion study shows
can be predicted \emph{a priori} from shoreline geometry \citep{PaperOne}.

What remains unresolved is the curve itself. Global databases report a capacity for every
large reservoir \citep{Lehner2011}, but it is the design figure carried from the dam's
records, the very number sedimentation invalidates. Observation-based storage products
largely track \emph{variations}, converting observed area into volume through a fitted or
pre-existing hypsometry \citep{BuskerEtAl2019}, so a stale curve propagates into the storage
estimate instead of being corrected by it. Where the curve is itself reconstructed, it is
typically inferred from terrain rather than measured on the reservoir, globally from a model
trained on the surrounding topography, to a mean absolute depth error near eight metres
\citep{Hao2024}, or at basin scale by interpolating a digital elevation model beneath the
observed extent \citep{BhatpuriaEtAl2024}. The alternative
is to measure the basin from the reservoir's own shorelines. \citet{Schwatke2020} did this by
stacking optical waterlines against nadir altimetry, which recovers the individual basin but
requires cloud-free imagery and a ground track that happens to cross the reservoir. Those two
requirements exclude the small, cloud-affected and off-track reservoirs where design curves
are oldest and gauges scarcest. It is not known whether substituting SAR for optical and
wide-swath for nadir altimetry lifts both requirements while still recovering a curve
accurate enough to operate on, nor how such a curve stands against a field survey.

The idea we pursue is that the shoreline exposed as a reservoir draws down forms a
sequence of contour lines at known water levels. Stacking Sentinel-1 waterlines ordered
by level reconstructs a digital elevation model (DEM) of the emerged basin, from which an
updated AEV curve and a storage time series follow by integration. The water level needed
to label each waterline is the single indispensable ingredient. Where a gauge exists this
is trivial; where it does not, the level must itself be remotely sensed. We therefore
treat the water-level source as a variable to be substituted, keeping the SAR area and
the reconstruction fixed and replacing the in-situ gauge with SWOT. Nadir altimetry, by
construction, only reaches the reservoirs a ground
track happens to cross; SWOT's wide swath observes a reservoir regardless of track,
addressing precisely the ungauged, track-missed case that orbit geometry alone excludes
from nadir altimetry.

The Mediterranean is among the regions where drought is projected to intensify most
\citep{IPCC2023, Cammalleri2022, Bevacqua2023}, and Italian water resources are already
under documented pressure \citep{ISPRA2023}. We exploit a shared regional
drought-to-refill event across Sicily in 2025--2026, in which most of our study
reservoirs drew down sharply and refilled within a few months. This produced a
short, temporally consistent image window in which each reservoir traversed an
unusually wide fraction of its observable elevation range, which we exploit with a
water-level-targeted sampling strategy (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).

We validate the reconstruction at nine Sicilian reservoirs against a
December-2025 echo-sounder survey, recent official bathymetric curves, a
water-level-substitution experiment testing whether SWOT reproduces gauge-based and
field-truth storage, and the water authority's own registered volume records. We further
quantify what the method can and cannot see. This means how much of each reservoir's total
storage the drawdown-exposed band represents, and how reconstruction reliability depends
on shoreline geometry and drawdown amplitude. The full pipeline is released as open-source
code, making both the method and its documented limits usable in practice.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the
nine Sicilian reservoirs and the SAR, water-level and ground-truth data used.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} defines the reconstruction method, covering how the SAR area and
water-level inputs are validated, the waterline-stacking DEM and scalar hypsometry, the
approach used to validate the resulting curves and bathymetry, the capacity-change metrics,
and how the method is applied in the released code. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the
validated inputs, the reconstructed bathymetry and capacity change, and the validation
against the echo-sounder survey, the official curves and the registered volume records.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the gauge-free, track-free implications, the
observability envelope, the role of shoreline geometry, and current limitations.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} summarises the main conclusions.
