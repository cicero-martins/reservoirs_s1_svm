\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reservoirs regulate water supply, irrigation, hydropower and flood risk, and their
operation depends on knowing how much water is stored as a function of level, i.e.\ the
area--elevation--volume (AEV) relationship. That relationship is not static: sediment
delivered by the contributing basin accumulates on the reservoir floor, progressively
reducing capacity. Yet updating it requires a bathymetric survey, and in-situ lake and
reservoir records worldwide are sparse, have if anything decreased in recent years, and,
even where they exist, are often not freely available for strategic or commercial
reasons \citep{BuskerEtAl2019}. Between one survey and the next, a reservoir is managed
with a stage record and whatever curve was last available, and any resulting
over-estimation of storage typically goes unquantified.

Satellite remote sensing offers a way to update this information without field
campaigns, because two complementary and freely available signals together contain
everything the AEV relationship requires. Satellite altimetry measures water-surface
\emph{elevation}: nadir radar missions, harmonised in databases such as DAHITI
\citep{Schwatke2015}, and, since 2022, the wide-swath mission SWOT
\citep{Biancamaria2016}. Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) measures water-surface
\emph{area} directly and, unlike optical sensors, is not affected by cloud cover or
solar illumination \citep{Torres2012, Mullissa2021}, a critical advantage in
Mediterranean and tropical contexts where cloud contamination can severely reduce the
temporal availability of optical observations \citep{Pekel2016}. The free, twice-weekly
Sentinel-1 archive \citep{Torres2012} delivers this signal with a reliability that a
companion study shows can be predicted \emph{a priori} from shoreline geometry
\citep{PaperOne}. Optical imagery remains just as fundamental, though, since its failure
modes (sun glint, turbidity, algae) are different from SAR's, giving an independent
perspective on the same water surface. \citet{Schwatke2020} combined optical water masks
with altimetry to reconstruct lake hypsometry; an optical implementation of the same
idea could in principle also reach reservoirs with neither an in-situ gauge nor an
altimetry ground track. Here we contribute a SAR-based route to that same large
population, adapting \citet{Schwatke2020}'s principle to Sentinel-1 waterlines.

The idea we pursue is that the shoreline exposed as a reservoir draws down is a sequence
of contour lines at known water levels: stacking Sentinel-1 waterlines ordered by level
reconstructs a digital elevation model (DEM) of the emerged basin, from which an updated
AEV curve and a storage time series follow by integration. The water level needed to
label each waterline is the single indispensable ingredient. Where a gauge exists this
is trivial; where it does not, the level must itself be remotely sensed. We therefore
treat the water-level source as a variable to be substituted: keeping the SAR area and
the reconstruction fixed, we replace the in-situ gauge first with nadir altimetry and
then with SWOT. Nadir altimetry, by construction, only reaches the reservoirs a ground
track happens to cross; SWOT's wide swath observes a reservoir regardless of track,
addressing precisely the ungauged, track-missed case that orbit geometry alone excludes
from nadir altimetry.

The contributions of this paper are: (i) a fully remote-sensing pipeline, adapting
\citet{Schwatke2020} to reservoirs with neither an in-situ gauge nor an altimetry
ground track, that reconstructs updated bathymetry and storage for the SAR-observable,
drawdown-exposed band from Sentinel-1 waterlines and a remote water level; (ii) three
converging lines of validation evidence for that reconstruction's accuracy: a
nine-reservoir comparison against a December-2025 echo-sounder survey and recent
official bathymetric curves, a water-level-substitution experiment showing SWOT
altimetry reproduces gauge-based and field-truth storage, and an independent PlanetScope
optical cross-sensor reconstruction; and (iii) an uncertainty and observability analysis
linking reconstruction reliability to shoreline geometry and drawdown amplitude,
released together with open-source code that makes the method and its limits usable in
practice.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes
the nine Sicilian reservoirs and the SAR, water-level, ground-truth and optical data
used. Section~\ref{sec:methods} details the waterline-stacking reconstruction, the
water-level- and mask-substitution validation experiments, the capacity-change and
uncertainty quantification, and the released code. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents
the reconstructed bathymetry and capacity change, the field and reference-curve
validation, the water-level-substitution and cross-sensor results, and the uncertainty
budget. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the gauge-free, track-free implications,
the observability envelope, the role of shoreline geometry, and current limitations.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} summarises the main conclusions.
