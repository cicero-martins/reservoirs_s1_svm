\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Reservoirs regulate water supply, irrigation, hydropower and flood risk
\citep{Pokhrel2021}, and their operation depends on knowing how much water is stored as a
function of level, i.e.\ the area--elevation--volume (AEV) relationship. That relationship
is not static. Sediment delivered by the contributing basin accumulates on the reservoir
floor, progressively reducing capacity, at a rate that has already cut global storage in
large reservoirs by several per cent and is projected to outpace new construction
\citep{Wisser2013}. Italy is a well-documented case, where half of fifty surveyed
reservoirs are expected to reach half their design capacity by 2050 \citep{Patro2022}.
Yet updating the AEV relationship requires a bathymetric survey, and in-situ lake and
reservoir records worldwide are sparse, have if anything decreased in recent years, and,
even where they exist, are often not freely available for strategic or commercial
reasons \citep{BuskerEtAl2019, Zhang2026}. Between one survey and the next, a reservoir is
managed with a stage record and whatever curve was last available, and any resulting
over-estimation of storage typically goes unquantified.

Satellite remote sensing offers a way to update this information without field
campaigns, because two complementary and freely available signals together contain
everything the AEV relationship requires. Satellite altimetry measures water-surface
\emph{elevation}, from nadir radar missions, harmonised in databases such as DAHITI
\citep{Schwatke2015}, to, since 2022, the wide-swath mission SWOT
\citep{Biancamaria2016}. Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) measures water-surface
\emph{area} directly and, unlike optical sensors, is not affected by cloud cover or
solar illumination \citep{Torres2012, Mullissa2021}, a critical advantage in
Mediterranean and tropical contexts where cloud contamination can severely reduce the
temporal availability of optical observations \citep{Pekel2016}. SAR water detection is
correspondingly well established, from operational flood mapping \citep{Martinis2015} to
systematic surface-water monitoring \citep{Bioresita2018, PenaLuque2021}. The free,
twice-weekly Sentinel-1 archive \citep{Torres2012}, processed at scale on platforms such
as Google Earth Engine \citep{Gorelick2017}, delivers this signal with a reliability that
a companion study shows can be predicted \emph{a priori} from shoreline geometry
\citep{PaperOne}. Reservoir extent and storage have accordingly been mapped globally from
space \citep{Lehner2011, Hao2024}, and combined satellite approaches are an active line of
work \citep{BhatpuriaEtAl2024, ValerioEtAl2024, PengEtAl2025, McQuillan2025}. Optical imagery remains just as fundamental, though, since its failure
modes (sun glint, turbidity, algae) are different from SAR's, giving an independent
perspective on the same water surface. \citet{Schwatke2020} combined optical water masks
with altimetry to reconstruct lake hypsometry, an approach made viable through
cloud-free optical imagery and nadir altimetry. This
raises a specific question. Can the same waterline-stacking principle be adapted to
SAR-derived water masks and wide-swath altimetry, and with what accuracy?

The idea we pursue, adapting \citet{Schwatke2020} to Sentinel-1 waterlines and SWOT, is
that the shoreline exposed as a reservoir draws down forms a
sequence of contour lines at known water levels. Stacking Sentinel-1 waterlines ordered
by level reconstructs a digital elevation model (DEM) of the emerged basin, from which an
updated AEV curve and a storage time series follow by integration. The water level needed
to label each waterline is the single indispensable ingredient. Where a gauge exists this
is trivial; where it does not, the level must itself be remotely sensed. We therefore
treat the water-level source as a variable to be substituted, keeping the SAR area and
the reconstruction fixed and replacing the in-situ gauge with SWOT. Nadir altimetry, by
construction, only reaches the reservoirs a ground
track happens to cross; SWOT's wide swath observes a reservoir regardless of track,
addressing precisely the ungauged, track-missed case that orbit geometry alone excludes
from nadir altimetry.

The Mediterranean is among the regions where drought is projected to intensify most
\citep{IPCC2023, Cammalleri2022, Bevacqua2023}, and Italian water resources are already
under documented pressure \citep{ISPRA2023}. We exploit a shared regional
drought-to-refill event across Sicily in 2025--2026, in which most of our study
reservoirs drew down sharply and refilled within a few months. This produced a
short, temporally consistent image window in which each reservoir traversed an
unusually wide fraction of its observable elevation range, which we exploit with a
water-level-targeted sampling strategy (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).

This paper's main contribution is a SAR-based adaptation of \citet{Schwatke2020}'s
waterline-stacking principle. It replaces optical imagery with Sentinel-1 water masks
and a nadir ground track with SWOT altimetry, reconstructing updated bathymetry and
storage for the SAR-observable, drawdown-exposed band of a reservoir without an in-situ
gauge or altimetry track. We validate it at nine Sicilian reservoirs against a
December-2025 echo-sounder survey, recent official bathymetric curves, a
water-level-substitution experiment testing whether SWOT reproduces gauge-based and
field-truth storage, and the water authority's own registered volume records. We further quantify what
the method can and cannot see. This means how much of each reservoir's total storage the
drawdown-exposed band represents, and how reconstruction reliability depends on
shoreline geometry and drawdown amplitude. The full pipeline is released as open-source
code, making both the method and its documented limits usable in practice.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the
nine Sicilian reservoirs and the SAR, water-level and ground-truth data used.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} defines the reconstruction method, covering how the SAR area and
water-level inputs are validated, the waterline-stacking DEM and scalar hypsometry, the
approach used to validate the resulting curves and bathymetry, the capacity-change metrics,
and how the method is applied in the released code. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the
validated inputs, the reconstructed bathymetry and capacity change, and the validation
against the echo-sounder survey, the official curves and the registered volume records.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the gauge-free, track-free implications, the
observability envelope, the role of shoreline geometry, and current limitations.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} summarises the main conclusions.
