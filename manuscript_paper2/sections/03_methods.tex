\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Validation of the SAR area and water-level inputs}
\label{sec:inputvalid}
The reconstruction below rests on two remotely sensed inputs, and both are validated
before use rather than assumed. Sentinel-1 water-surface area comes from the
scene-adaptive classifier of \citet{PaperOne}, whose reliability against independent
truth, and its dependence on shoreline area-to-perimeter ratio (A/P), is established
there; we do not re-derive it here, only apply it (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

The water level is the other required input, and where a gauge is unavailable it must
itself be remotely sensed. We validate this substitution in two separate steps rather
than as one combined figure. First, \emph{as a water level}: SWOT is compared directly
to the in-situ gauge, independent of any SAR area or reconstruction, giving a per-reservoir
water-level RMSE with no volume computed yet (Section~\ref{sec:res_inputvalid}). Only once
that establishes where SWOT is an acceptable substitute do we ask what carrying it through
the reconstruction does to \emph{storage}: keeping the SAR area fixed, we pair it with the
in-situ gauge and, separately, with SWOT altimetry, fit the power-law hypsometry of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:powerlaw} to each, and compare the resulting integrated storage to each other
and to field/survey truth (Section~\ref{sec:res_valid}). Because SWOT's wide swath observes
a reservoir regardless of ground track, whereas nadir altimetry only reaches the reservoirs
a track happens to cross, this validates the water-level substitution that later lets the
whole pipeline run without either a gauge or an altimetry ground track.

The same substitution is validated a second time, directly at the level of the spatial
reconstruction rather than only the volume it integrates to. Every waterline mask's
gauge-derived level is replaced with one inferred purely from SWOT: the power-law model
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:powerlaw}) is fit on genuine SAR-area/SWOT-level coincidences drawn from the
full SWOT record, then inverted at each mask's own observed area, so no gauge enters the
substitution at any point. At Poma this reproduces the gauge-based DEM to $0.31$~m RMSE
($0.06$~m bias) over the entire basin, with no pixel differing by more than $1$~m
(Section~\ref{sec:res_ladder}). The reconstruction itself, and not only the storage it
yields, is therefore not gauge-dependent.

\subsection{Bathymetric reconstruction: DEM and scalar hypsometry}
\label{sec:dem}

  \label{fig:methodflow}

With both inputs validated, the reconstruction follows. Let $\{M_i\}$ be the Sentinel-1
binary water masks and $\{h_i\}$ their water levels, sorted ascending by $h_i$. Pixels
wet in the smallest mask (the always-wet core) are assigned the lowest level $h_0$. A
pixel that first becomes wet between consecutive levels is a shoreline exposed in that
increment and is assigned the mid-level $(h_{i-1}+h_i)/2$. The resulting elevation field
is closed over the union footprint, interior gaps are filled by linear interpolation
(\texttt{scipy.griddata}), and the DEM is smoothed with a Gaussian filter ($\sigma=2$
px, $=20$~m). This adapts \citet{Schwatke2020} to Sentinel-1 waterlines. The procedure
is agnostic to the mask source: any sequence of binary water masks with associated
water levels stacks identically, which we exploit to cross-validate the reconstruction
with an independent optical sensor (Section~\ref{sec:xsensor}). The DEM reconstructs
only the drawdown-\emph{exposed} band $[\,\text{floor},\text{max}\,]$; the permanently
wet deep zone is not observed.

As an alternative that needs only the area series, each SAR area is instead paired
with the nearest water level (within $\pm10$ days) and a power-law hypsometry is
fitted,
\begin{equation}
  A(h) = a\,(h-h_0)^{b},
\label{eq:powerlaw}
\end{equation}
which is integrated to storage $V(h)=\int A\,\mathrm{d}h$. The DEM gives the spatial
bathymetry and change maps; the scalar fit gives a robust storage curve where the
observed level range is wide. Both see only the exposed band.

The mask stack $\{M_i\}$ need not be limited to the dates used to fit
Eq.~\eqref{eq:powerlaw}. Within the same reconstruction window, additional Sentinel-1
acquisitions are typically available at $2$--$4\times$ the density of the stratified
set; each contributes one further waterline once assigned a level by the same
source-priority rule (Section~\ref{sec:inputvalid}), falling back to Eq.~\eqref{eq:powerlaw},
inverted at that date's own observed area, only for the few dates neither gauge nor SWOT
reaches. Because the fit itself is calibrated solely on the original gauge/SWOT pairs,
this densification adds spatial detail to the DEM without feeding back into the curve it
is validated against. At Poma, expanding the stack from $10$ to $24$ masks roughly halved
the tallest single-elevation terrace step (one level otherwise shared by some $2\,000$
pixels) while changing the reconstructed elevation by less than $1$~m over $97\%$ of the
basin.

Masks entering either stack are screened by two complementary criteria. The first,
inherited from \citet{PaperOne}'s own area time series, flags any date whose observed
area deviates by more than $60\%$ from its temporal neighbours, catching gross
classification failures. The second, added here, sorts the candidate masks by water
level and flags any date whose area \emph{decreases} while the paired level rises --
a physically inconsistent step for a monotonically filling or draining reservoir that a
purely magnitude-based threshold can miss. The two are not redundant: at Rosamarina, a
single mask with a salt-and-pepper misclassification pattern (consistent with
wind-roughening of the water surface on that acquisition) reduced the exposed area by
only $15\%$ relative to its neighbours, well under the deviation threshold, yet was
caught immediately by the monotonicity check and excluded before reconstruction.

\subsection{Validation of the reconstructed curves and bathymetry}
\label{sec:xsensor}
The reconstruction itself is then checked against two further independent references,
both restricted to the SAR-observable band, the only part either reference and the
reconstruction can be compared over. The DEM is compared to Garcia's echo-sounder
survey, the one genuine field ground truth, as an elevation-wise bias and RMSE and as
a band-relative storage-loss comparison. For the six reservoirs with a recent official
area--volume curve, the DEM's own band capacity change is compared to that curve's
band and total capacity change relative to the original design curve, so both figures
are on the same footing (\% of design capacity) rather than one in area and one in
volume.

Because the reconstruction (Section~\ref{sec:dem}) is agnostic to the mask source, we
additionally rebuild the bathymetry from PlanetScope 3~m optical water masks and
compare it, pixel-wise and in AEV, to the Sentinel-1 reconstruction over the four
reservoirs PlanetScope covers (2024--2025). Water is delineated per scene by a
normalised difference water index,
$\mathrm{NDWI}=(\rho_\text{green}-\rho_\text{NIR})/(\rho_\text{green}+\rho_\text{NIR})$,
thresholded per scene. As with the water-level substitution above, each site is
labelled from one of two deliberately independent tiers, the in-situ gauge by default,
or SWOT where the gauge is degenerate over the optical window (chosen automatically
from the gauge's interquartile range over the mask dates, $<0.5$~m indicating a stuck
sensor), to avoid mixing datums within a single reconstruction. This provides an
independent, higher-resolution optical check and, for \emph{Ancipa} and
\emph{Pozzillo} (which lack a modern bathymetric survey), an independent near-truth.
Because optical detection carries error modes orthogonal to SAR (surface algae and
macrophytes, turbidity, sun glint), agreement between the two reconstructions is
strong evidence that neither is dominated by sensor-specific error.

\subsection{Capacity-change metrics and uncertainty}
\label{sec:uncertainty}
Capacity change relative to the design curve is quantified two ways: \emph{band}
(relative to the DEM floor, i.e.\ the SAR-observable exposed zone, the fair
comparison against survey truth) and \emph{total} (the full design curve, including
the deep zone below the floor that SAR cannot see). Because sedimentation concentrates
in the deep zone, the SAR band change is a validated \emph{lower bound} on total loss.
We additionally quantify how much of each reservoir's total capacity the exposed band
represents, using the design curve's own volume at the DEM floor as the deep-zone
estimate: every core reservoir's design curve is tabulated down to at or near zero
volume, i.e.\ its own lowest surveyed point is effectively the pre-impoundment valley
floor, so this estimate is read directly from data already in use rather than requiring
a new terrain source.

The one field-validated reservoir (Garcia) yields a normalised, elevation-dependent
bias model $\mathrm{bias}(f)$, $f=(z-\text{floor})/(\text{max}-\text{floor})$, applied
to the other reservoirs. Two distinct uncertainties follow from it. The first is
calibration uncertainty in the bias model itself: we draw 500 samples of its two
regression coefficients from their joint covariance and propagate each through the
band-capacity-change calculation, giving a 5th--95th-percentile confidence interval
that is narrow ($\pm1.3\%$ median) because it reflects only the fitted line's own
uncertainty, not independent per-pixel noise. The second, larger, uncertainty is
whether the bias model \emph{transfers} to other reservoirs at all: the RMS of the
bias-corrected error against independent truth at Rosamarina and Poma (excluding the
self-calibrated Garcia) is $\pm8.5$ percentage points, the dominant term in
the overall budget. This error, consistent with the shoreline-geometry dependence
already established for the SAR area input itself, is what any operational use of the
reconstruction must budget for.

\subsection{Application: updating the monitoring tool}
\label{sec:tool}
With the inputs, the reconstruction and its outputs all validated within a stated
uncertainty, the resulting AEV curves replace the design curve, over the
SAR-observable band, in a reservoir-monitoring tool. The full pipeline (Earth Engine
mask export, water-level ingestion, DEM reconstruction, and AEV/storage integration)
is released as open-source Python code, together with a Streamlit
application that displays, for a selected reservoir, the reconstructed 2D/3D
bathymetry, the AEV curves against design and survey references, and the multi-period
change map, and offers the DEM for download. The application currently runs locally
from the released code rather than as a hosted service. Because every step, from SAR
classification to water-level substitution, runs on freely available satellite data,
the same pipeline is directly transferable to any reservoir with a comparable
drawdown-exposed range, without field access.
