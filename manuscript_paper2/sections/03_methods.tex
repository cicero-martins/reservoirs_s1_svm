\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Validation of the SAR area and water-level inputs}
\label{sec:inputvalid}
Sentinel-1 water-surface area comes from the scene-adaptive classifier validated in
\citet{PaperOne}. That companion study establishes, across 62 reservoirs on five
continents, that classification accuracy can be predicted \emph{a priori} from the
shoreline area-to-perimeter ratio (A/P). The boundary strip of ambiguous pixels scales
with shoreline length rather than water area, so a mixed-pixel argument predicts
increasing reliability as A/P grows, and this prediction holds empirically against
independent truth.

For the water-level input, SWOT is compared to the in-situ gauge, giving a per-reservoir
water-level RMSE (Section~\ref{sec:res_inputvalid}). SWOT is first screened for outliers,
then matched to the gauge record within a short time tolerance. SWOT and the gauge are
not guaranteed to share a common reference, so a per-reservoir constant shift, estimated
once from their overlap, is removed from SWOT before this comparison, correcting a
systematic offset rather than a genuine difference between the two records' levels.

Agreement is reported as both RMSE and the Kling--Gupta efficiency (KGE)
\citep{Gupta2009}, the same skill score used throughout \citet{PaperOne}, so the two
studies' notions of agreement are directly comparable. KGE decomposes skill into three
components, the correlation $r$, the variability ratio $\alpha$ (ratio of standard
deviations) and the mean ratio $\beta$, combined as
$\mathrm{KGE}=1-\sqrt{(r-1)^2+(\alpha-1)^2+(\beta-1)^2}$, with $1$ a perfect score.
Reporting the components alongside the total matters here because they separate an
offset or a variance mismatch from a genuine failure to track the signal. One caveat
applies throughout: because these series are absolute elevations rather than anomalies,
$\beta$ sits at essentially $1.00$ everywhere and is a weak discriminator at this scale.

\subsection{Bathymetric reconstruction: DEM and scalar hypsometry}
\label{sec:dem}
With both inputs validated, the reconstruction follows. Let $\{M_i\}$ be the Sentinel-1
binary water masks and $\{h_i\}$ their water levels, sorted ascending by $h_i$. We adapt
\citet{Schwatke2020}'s principle of stacking waterlines ordered by level to Sentinel-1
masks, where each pixel's bed elevation is set, in principle, by the level at which the
mask sequence switches from dry to wet. Real SAR masks are not perfectly nested, though.
A narrow, deep channel can be misclassified as land at low water and only detected again
at a higher level, so a pixel can flicker between wet and dry rather than switching
once. We handle this with our own refinement, choosing, for each pixel, the split level
that minimises the disagreement between its observed wet/dry sequence and a single
clean transition, rather than taking the last dry-to-wet switch at face value. This
reduces to the plain level-slice rule when the masks are nested and stays robust when
they are not. The reservoir footprint is defined from persistence, pixels wet in at
least $15\%$ of masks, which keeps disconnected in-reservoir pools, such as a near-dam
pocket isolated at low water, while rejecting transient or external water; its boundary
is fixed to the highest observed shoreline, so the reconstruction is a closed,
bathtub-shaped basin. Remaining gaps inside the footprint are filled by interpolation,
and the whole DEM is smoothed with a Gaussian filter ($\sigma=2$~px, $=20$~m). The
procedure is agnostic to the mask source. Any sequence of binary water masks with
associated water levels stacks identically. The DEM
reconstructs only the drawdown-\emph{exposed} band $[\,\text{floor},\text{max}\,]$; the
permanently wet deep zone is not observed.

The same paired masks and levels also fit a scalar power-law hypsometry,
\begin{equation}
  A(h) = a\,(h-h_0)^{b},
\label{eq:powerlaw}
\end{equation}
integrated to storage $V(h)=\int A\,\mathrm{d}h$. This scalar fit gives a robust storage
curve on its own, and inverting it at a mask's own observed area assigns that mask a
water level even when no gauge or SWOT observation falls near its acquisition date.
This is what lets the mask stack extend beyond the dates used to calibrate the fit in
the first place. Sentinel-1 acquires considerably more often than SWOT overpasses the
same reservoir, giving a median of $3.3\times$ as many distinct dates across the nine
reservoirs within the reconstruction window (range $1.9$--$5.8\times$), so a substantial
surplus of masks is available for densification. Each contributes one further waterline
once assigned a level, either directly by the same source-priority rule as
Section~\ref{sec:inputvalid}, or, for the few dates neither gauge nor SWOT reaches, by
this inversion. Because the fit itself is calibrated solely on the original gauge/SWOT
pairs, this densification adds spatial detail to the DEM without feeding back into the
curve it is validated against.

Masks entering either stack are screened by two complementary criteria. The first,
inherited from \citet{PaperOne}'s own area time series, flags any date whose observed
area deviates by more than $60\%$ from its temporal neighbours, catching gross
classification failures. The second, added here, sorts the candidate masks by water
level and flags any date whose area \emph{decreases} while the paired level rises, a
physically inconsistent step for a monotonically filling or draining reservoir that a
purely magnitude-based threshold can miss. The two are not redundant.

\subsection{Validation of the reconstructed curves and bathymetry}
\label{sec:curvevalid}
The reconstruction itself is then checked against two further independent references,
both restricted to the SAR-observable band, the only part either reference and the
reconstruction can be compared over. The DEM is compared to Garcia's echo-sounder
survey, the one genuine field ground truth, as an elevation-wise bias and RMSE and as
a band-relative storage-loss comparison. For the six reservoirs with a recent official
area--volume curve, the DEM's own band capacity change is compared to that curve's
band and total capacity change relative to the original design curve, so both figures
are on the same footing (\% of design capacity) rather than one in area and one in
volume.

Capacity change relative to the design curve is quantified two ways. The \emph{band}
change is measured relative to the DEM floor, that is over the SAR-observable exposed
zone, and is the fair comparison against survey truth. The \emph{total} change refers to
the full design curve, including the deep zone below the floor that SAR cannot see.
Because sedimentation concentrates in the deep zone, the SAR band change is a validated
\emph{lower bound} on total loss. We additionally quantify how much of each reservoir's
total capacity the exposed band represents, using the design curve's own volume at the
DEM floor as the deep-zone estimate. Every core reservoir's design curve is tabulated
down to at or near zero volume, so its own lowest surveyed point is effectively the
pre-impoundment valley floor, and this estimate is read directly from data already in
use rather than requiring a new terrain source.
\label{sec:capmetrics}

\subsection{Application: updating the monitoring tool}
\label{sec:tool}
With the inputs, the reconstruction and its outputs all validated within a stated
uncertainty, the resulting AEV curves replace the design curve, over the
SAR-observable band, in a reservoir-monitoring tool. The full pipeline (Earth Engine
mask export, water-level ingestion, DEM reconstruction, and AEV/storage integration)
is released as open-source Python code on GitHub, together with a Streamlit
application, deployed online, that displays, for a selected reservoir, the
reconstructed 2D/3D bathymetry, the AEV curves against design and survey references,
and the multi-period change map, and offers the DEM for download.
