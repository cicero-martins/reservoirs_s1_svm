\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Gauge-free, track-free storage}
The water-level-substitution result (Section~\ref{sec:res_hyps}) shows that
replacing the in-situ gauge with SWOT altimetry changes reconstructed band storage by
$6\%$ on average across the nine reservoirs, with a median of $2.4\%$; the mean is
carried by Olivo ($28\%$), the lowest-A/P case in the set. Because SWOT observes by swath rather than along a
nadir track, it removes the two constraints that keep small reservoirs off nadir
altimetry's map: the need for a gauge, and the need for a satellite ground track to
actually cross the water body. Notably, DAHITI could add these small reservoirs only
once SWOT existed, so wide-swath altimetry is what makes them observable at all.
Rosamarina makes the case concretely. Its gauge suffered two separate stuck episodes
at two different flat readings, spanning ${\sim}19$ months combined across
2024--2026, and only the SWOT level recovered the true drawdown; without it, the
reconstruction over that window collapsed to a flat, unphysical surface
(Section~\ref{sec:res_inputvalid}). Pozzillo makes a related but distinct case.
Its gauge combines a 13-month frozen episode of its own with a persistent
range-compression across the rest of the record, and independently-measured SAR area
corroborates SWOT's wider water-level range over the gauge's narrower one throughout
(Section~\ref{sec:res_inputvalid}); the highest-A/P reservoir in the set nonetheless
needed the same substitution, a reminder that gauge reliability and shoreline
geometry are separate axes, not the same risk. For Rosamarina and Pozzillo, the
satellite source was not merely as good as the gauge but strictly better. Ancipa
illustrates a different lesson. Its raw SWOT record first appeared to degrade sharply
at this low-A/P reservoir, but the divergence traced entirely to three dates where
SWOT's lake detection failed as the water body shrank to a sliver during extreme
drawdown (Section~\ref{sec:res_ladder}); once excluded, SWOT agrees with the gauge as
well as anywhere in this study ($r=1.00$). A satellite record that looks unreliable at
low A/P can therefore fail for a specific, detectable, geometry-linked reason rather
than a blanket one. Nicoletti, where SWOT retains real residual scatter beyond a
confirmed gauge freeze ($\alpha=1.32$, Table~\ref{tab:wlrmse}), is the closer analogue
to a case needing that scrutiny. Which source is more reliable is therefore
reservoir-, and sometimes date-, specific, not a fixed property of either technology.

\subsection{What the method sees: the observability envelope}
The reconstruction is bounded by the drawdown-exposed band. A reservoir that draws
down close to its dead pool, or that is filling from empty, exposes most of its basin
and can be reconstructed almost completely; a reservoir kept near full exposes only a
thin upper band. Consequently, deep-zone sedimentation is invisible to SAR and the
band capacity change is a lower bound on total loss (Section~\ref{sec:res_change}).
The 2025--2026 Sicilian drought-to-refill event, by drawing most reservoirs down
sharply and refilling them within months, was particularly favourable to the method,
exposing an unusually wide elevation range in a short, temporally consistent window
(Section~\ref{sec:intro}). Because every step, from SAR classification to
water-level substitution, runs on freely available satellite data, the same pipeline
is directly transferable to any reservoir with a comparable drawdown-exposed range,
without field access.

\subsection{Reliability scales with A/P}
The area-to-perimeter ratio that predicts SAR water-area reliability
\citep{PaperOne} also predicts bathymetric-reconstruction reliability. Narrow,
low-A/P reservoirs (Olivo, Ancipa, Nicoletti) show the largest apparent capacity
losses in Section~\ref{sec:res_change} and, where an independent curve exists
(Nicoletti), the widest gap between SAR and truth (Section~\ref{sec:res_curves}). The
Ancipa's SWOT water level is \emph{not} a further example of this degradation.
Once three geometry-linked bad dates are excluded (Section~\ref{sec:res_ladder}), it
agrees with the gauge as well as anywhere in the study, a reminder that low A/P
degrades some remote-sensing signals at a given reservoir but not necessarily all of
them, and each source needs its own check rather than a blanket presumption.
Independent lines of evidence, the apparent capacity loss and the curve-validation gap,
therefore converge on the same low-A/P reservoirs, so A/P offers an a-priori screen for
where the fully remote pipeline can be trusted, though which \emph{specific} input
degrades there is not automatic and must be checked reservoir by reservoir.

Two of those signatures are worth reading together, because they say the same thing
about the low-A/P reservoirs from opposite directions. Where the reconstructed
volume--elevation curve sits left of \emph{both} the design and the updated curve at
every level while those two stay close to each other (Section~\ref{sec:res_dem}), the
displacement is more readily explained by an under-reconstructed DEM than by a basin
that has genuinely lost that much storage, since a real loss would move the updated
curve away from the design curve rather than leaving both behind.

Nicoletti and Castello are the reservoirs whose hypsometric curve is calibrated over the
narrowest slice of their observed water-level range, and the requirement that a SAR
acquisition coincide with a SWOT overpass within $\pm3$ days is what narrows it. A curve
fitted mostly near full pool and then applied through a full drawdown will misstate
storage at low levels, so this is a coverage limitation that a longer SWOT record should
relax rather than a defect of the gauge-free chain.

\subsection{Systematic bias, and why we do not correct for it}
The elevation-dependent bias (Section~\ref{sec:res_garcia}) is intrinsic to the
level-slicing reconstruction, but one field calibration is a thin basis for correcting
eight other reservoirs, so we report the raw, uncorrected estimate throughout and
budget instead for its own ${\sim}10\%$ disagreement with independent truth at Garcia's
A/P and above. More field surveys, ideally spanning a range of sedimentation histories
rather than one more confirmatory case, would be needed before any part of this bias
could be treated as reservoir-general.

\subsection{Limitations and future work}
Four limitations bound the present results. First, the SWOT record begins only in mid
2023, so the fully-remote storage series is still short; this will lengthen with the
mission. Second, the validation sample is small (nine reservoirs, one echo-sounder
survey), although the water-level-substitution experiment partly compensates by adding
independent evidence on the same reservoirs. Third, and most fundamental, the deep
permanently-wet zone is never exposed and so is invisible to any waterline method; where a
modern survey exists it can be added to close the AEV curve, but in general the SAR
estimate remains a lower bound on total capacity loss.

Fourth, the operational comparison of Section~\ref{sec:res_voltime} is a test of internal
consistency rather than of absolute accuracy. Inverting the independent reference curves at
the registered volumes shows the SAR area falling $10$--$25\%$ short of the area those
curves require, at four of the five reservoirs where the check is possible. Some of that
gap is footprint rather than classification, since Ancipa's reconstruction ceiling
($69.5$~ha) sits below the maximum its own SAR record reaches ($89.3$~ha). Because the
hypsometric curve is fitted on these same areas, it absorbs whatever bias they carry.
Agreement with the operational record therefore shows the gauge-free chain is self-consistent
end to end, but cannot by itself separate an error in the curve from an error in the area
driving it. Disentangling the two needs an area reference independent of our own
classification, which the echo-sounder survey supplies at one reservoir only.

Three avenues would strengthen the validation directly. A field campaign combining an
uncrewed surface vehicle (echo-sounding the deep zone) with drone photogrammetry (the
exposed margin) would yield a complete, independently-measured basin DEM, turning the
lower bound into a measured total and extending the bias calibration beyond the single
Garcia survey; the low-A/P reservoirs (Ancipa) and a sedimenting mid-A/P case
(Rosamarina) are the most informative targets. Separately, Sentinel-1 interferometry
over the \emph{exposed margin} could refine waterline delineation (interferometric
coherence) and probe compaction of freshly-exposed sediment (differential InSAR); we
stress that interferometry cannot retrieve open-water level, because the water surface
decorrelates between passes, so it is a complement to the margin geometry, not a
water-level source. Third, Ancipa's specific SWOT gap during extreme drawdown
(Section~\ref{sec:res_ladder}) points to a concrete data-level fix rather than only a
methodological caveat. The LakeSP product we use aggregates the finer-grained Pixel
Cloud (L2\_HR\_PIXC) into one integrated level per lake per pass, and produces nothing
at all if too few pixels are classified as a coherent water feature, exactly what
happens once a reservoir shrinks to a sliver. Reprocessing the Pixel Cloud directly
(via its PIXCVec attribute file, which tags individual pixels to a Prior Lake Database
feature even when the aggregate LakeSP entry fails) could recover a usable water level
for exactly these extreme-drawdown gaps, at the cost of granule-level processing in
place of the Hydrocron API calls used throughout this study; we did not pursue this
here since the three affected Ancipa dates were already resolved by exclusion, but it
is a direct route to close this class of gap in future work, particularly for
reservoirs where the drawdown-exposed band leaves the water body smaller than the
LakeSP product can reliably resolve.
